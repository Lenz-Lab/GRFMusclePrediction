Metrics

what the numbers on this site are actually asking.

Reconstruction accuracy

How closely a model's predicted force/moment curves match the ground-truth (static-optimization) curves for the same trial.

RRMSE (weighted) Root-mean-square error, normalized by each output's signal range and averaged with larger-range outputs weighted more heavily. 0 is perfect; lower is better. The default headline metric across the site because it stays well-behaved even on low-variance outputs, unlike R².
R² Fraction of variance in the ground truth explained by the prediction. 1.0 is perfect, 0 means "no better than predicting the mean," and it can go arbitrarily negative on a small or low-variance test set — a single atypical held-out subject can drag it far below zero without the model actually being bad. Report alongside RRMSE, not instead of it.
Dice coefficient Continuous-curve overlap between predicted and true signals (borrowed from image-segmentation overlap metrics, adapted for non-negative force curves). 1.0 is perfect overlap.
AUC (integral agreement) Trapezoidal overlap coefficient: integral of min(true, predicted) over integral of max(true, predicted), bounded [0, 1] with 1.0 = perfect overlap. Conceptually close to Dice but a distinct construction (integrates over time rather than summing samples pointwise — see eval_utils.calc_auc_per_output), kept separate rather than treated as a rescaled Dice. On the Predict page, the shaded region between the ground-truth and predicted curves is the visual version of this same question — how much of the two curves actually overlap — for outputs in the standard reported subset (see below); it isn't a pixel-exact rendering of the trapezoidal ratio, just the same idea made visible.
MAE Mean absolute error, in the signal's native units (N or N/kg). 0 is perfect.
Why both R² and RRMSE?

They can disagree, and when they do, RRMSE is the one to trust. R² normalizes by signal variance, so on a compressed or low-variance test set (a single atypical subject, a retention-stratum trial) it can swing to large negative values that look catastrophic but reflect the test set's variance, not the model's accuracy. RRMSE normalizes by signal range instead, so it stays well-behaved in exactly the cases where R² gets fragile. Both are reported because they answer different questions; RRMSE is the tiebreaker.

Every result also distinguishes the full output set (all muscles/joints the model predicts, signals.outputs in config.yaml) from the reported subset (reporting.primary_outputs, or a version-specific override like V1_outputs — see a result's subset field). The subset exists because not every predicted output is part of this project's clinical narrative: addmagMid (adductor magnus, middle segment), for instance, is computed and available in every per-output breakdown, but isn't part of the standard reported subset — it isn't relevant to the knee/ankle JRF or gastroc:soleus questions this project is actually built around. Per-output charts still show it; the subset just isn't built to headline it.

Cross-stratum transfer & ratio recovery

Uhlrich et al. (2022) found a biofeedback intervention shifted the gastrocnemius:soleus force ratio between baseline and retention — less gastroc, more soleus, after training. Can a model reproduce that shift?

Three training regimes are compared: base-trained and ret-trained each see one condition and are evaluated cold on the other; pooled-trained sees both, so it has no cold-transfer gap and serves as a reference point. All three estimate the same shift — they aren't a direction pair like the reconstruction table above (pooled-trained has no mirror row).

RRMSE staying flat across conditions doesn't mean the ratio shift survived — a model can reconstruct curves fine while missing the shift entirely. That's what the stats below check.

gasmed-only, not "gastrocnemius" generally

Elsewhere on this site (per-output charts, the full output set) you'll see gaslat and gasmed as two separate muscle outputs, and sometimes a combined "gastrocnemius" figure that sums both heads. The ratio used here is not that combined figure — it's gasmed (medial gastrocnemius) alone, matching Uhlrich et al.'s Eq. 3 and Gastroc_Soleus_Activation_Ratio.ipynb's ground truth exactly. Every saved transfer result carries its own ratio_definition string stating this explicitly — check it before comparing ratio numbers across files, rather than assuming "gastrocnemius" means the same thing everywhere on the site.

Pearson r Linear correlation between each subject's true ratio shift and the model's predicted shift. Sensitive to outliers and assumes a roughly linear relationship.
Spearman ρ Rank correlation only — does a subject with a bigger true shift also get a bigger predicted shift, regardless of exact scale? More robust than Pearson with few subjects, which is why it leads on this site (these experiments run on n=10 subjects — take any single p-value with real caution, and lean on Spearman over Pearson).
bias & limits of agreement Bland-Altman style: bias is the average signed difference (predicted − true) across subjects — positive means the model systematically under-predicts the shift, i.e. pulls toward "no change" rather than random noise. The limits of agreement (loa_lower/loa_upper) are the range that ~95% of individual subject errors fall within — a measure of scatter around that bias, not just its average.
spread ratio std(predicted shift) ÷ std(true shift) across subjects — a completely separate question from correlation. 1.0 means the model reproduces the true shift's magnitude faithfully; well below 1.0 means shifts are compressed toward the group average even if they're correctly ranked. A model can have excellent Spearman ρ and a poor spread ratio at the same time — that's not a contradiction, it's two different failure/success modes coexisting.
transfer cost Two cold-transfer costs, at different levels. Δrrmse_w: reconstruction error cost of testing on an unseen condition (rrmse_w_base2ret − rrmse_w_ret2ret). Δbias (ratio_transfer_cost_bias): the same idea for the ratio shift — base-trained.bias − ret-trained.bias. Neither pairs with the reconstruction table's direction rows; both compare two independently cold-generalizing models against each other.

Two worked examples from a real run

LSTM+Attention, base-trained: Spearman ρ=0.96 (p<0.0001) — near-perfect ranking — but spread ratio=0.32, so the predicted shift is compressed to about a third of true size. Strong rank recovery, weak magnitude fidelity.

CNN-LSTM: correlation near zero across all three regimes, not significant — a genuine null result, marked n.s. rather than hidden.
